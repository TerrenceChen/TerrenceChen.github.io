I am a political, cultural, and historical sociologist studying patterns of political contestation and public discourse in democratic societies. Using computational text analysis, large language models, historical archival methods, qualitative in-depth interviews, and statistical methods, I explore how political parties construct governing visions that constrain democratic possibilities, as well as how public discourse and civic engagement expand and challenge the official boundaries of politics. 

My dissertation, *Fiscalization of Politics: Budget Talk as Political Justification in the U.S., 1921–2024*, examines how American politicians use discourse about budget deficits, public debt, and taxpayer money to debate and justify public policy. Using large language models and historical archival data, I explain why Democrats have increasingly embraced fiscal responsibility since the 1970s, while Republicans have become more opportunistic in their budget talk --- emphasizing budget concerns primarily when Democrats are in power and de-emphasizing them when they control the executive branch.

My second stream of research examines how public discourse constructs political divisions. In a paper forthcoming in *Sociological Science*, I develop a measure of "politicization," or the process through which issues become associated with recognizable political divisions in public discourse, with à la carte word embeddings. Analyzing 1.35 million mainstream news media articles from 1980 to 2024, I find that the boundary of politics has expanded substantially, while its substantive content has been continually renegotiated: race and immigration, gender, and legal institutions have become the most politicized topics in American society, while issues like the home and financial markets have remained comparatively insulated from politics. In so doing, I challenge how we think about “politics” as a fundamental category in social science: rather than treating the meaning of politics as stable, I show how the boundary and content of politics are constantly redefined in different historical periods.

My previous work on Taiwan's digital democracy focuses on how Taiwan as a young democracy started to promote digital tools to facilitate democratic participation, as well as the conditions under which these new tools allow citizens to influence policy changes. My work has been published in [Third World Quarterly](https://doi.org/10.1080/01436597.2022.2042801), [Big Data & Society](https://doi.org/10.1177/20539517241296038), and [Information, Communication & Society](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2025.2558150).
