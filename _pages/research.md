## The Fiscalization of Politics: Budget Talk as Political Justification in U.S. Congress, 1921–2024

*Dissertation Project (ASA Dissertation Development Research Improvement Grant)*

**Question**: Why did fiscal responsibility become a durable component of Democratic political identity precisely when Republicans were moving away from it?

Using large language models to analyze over 8 million congressional floor speeches alongside archival evidence, I trace how fiscal responsibility became a durable feature of Democratic political identity. I show that this endured not because Democrats became neoliberals, but because liberal Democrats articulated a version of fiscal responsibility — what I term "responsible governance liberalism" — that complemented rather than contradicted commitments to social protection and government activism. In addition, fiscal responsibility functioned as a "coalition magnet" in the Demcoratic Party, which draws together otherwise competing factions within the party around a shared governing vision and giving Democrats a language for criticizing Republican governing records. The dissertation traces the emergence of these conditions in the 1970s and 1980s, then examines their mobilization in two pivotal legislative episodes: the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1993 and the Statutory Pay-As-You-Go (PAYGO) Act of 2010.


## Tracing Politicization in U.S. Public Discourse, 1980-2024

*Conditionally Accepted at Sociological Science*

**Question**: How has American public discourse become more politicized?

Using word embedding models trained on 1.35 million newspaper articles, I develop a new measure of discursive politicization and trace its trajectory across 131 issue areas between 1980 and 2024. Politicization has increased substantially over the past four decades and expanded beyond traditional political issues into many areas of social life. The perceived center of political conflict has also shifted, with race and immigration, gender and family, and legal institutions becoming more politicized than economic issues. This paper challenges how we think about “politics” as a fundamental category in social science: rather than treating the meaning of politics as stable, I show how the boundary and content of politics are constantly redefined in different historical periods.


## Mapping the Relationship between Public and Personal Culture: The Case of "Woke" in America

*Working Paper (with Jintae Bae, Sakeef Karim, and Milos Brocic)*

**Question**: How are public narratives around "wokeness" aligned or misaligned with personal understandings of it?

This project combines the American Culture War Survey with a computational analysis of five mainstream media outlets from 2015 to 2025 to examine the relationship between public narratives and personal culture. We find a significant mismatch between the "public" understanding of "woke," as reflected in elite newspaper coverage, and the "personal" understanding of the term, as reflected in our open-ended survey responses.
